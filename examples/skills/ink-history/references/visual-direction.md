# Visual direction: drawing, staging and comic timing

## The picture language

This is a widescreen illustrated comedy, not an infographic, a puppet theatre or a vertical short. A narrator explains history while characters enact the emotional cost of the explanation. The cut, the character's thought and the narrator's emphasized word usually land together. Draw a new reaction for each thought; a stock smile with a talking mouth does not carry the format.

The studied film uses large white bean heads and thick warm-black ink, saturated local colour, cel shadows, painted perspective settings and limited animation. These are craft observations, not permission to copy its cast or art. All character silhouettes, stories, sets and compositions in a new film must be original.

## Figures and line

- A plain white rounded skull, no nose or ears. Bean/dot eyes occupy little of the face. Leave a generous blank chin. Identity comes from hat/hair silhouette, facial hair, clothes, build and posture. Keep faces white in daylight; night can tint the whole picture blue-violet. Avoid a creamy base face that erases the contrast with the shadow.
- A near-black warm contour around every solid form, roughly 6–8 pixels on full figures and 10–16 on close heads at 1920-wide delivery. Interior lines taper and are often thinner. Use a few decisive curves and sharp corners; do not sprinkle random wobble or noise over every edge. Uniformly thin SVG-like lines lose the weight.
- Hard-edged mauve-grey shadow crescents on white faces, a gentle gradient inside the shadow, bright rims where motivated. Clothes/hat/hair get two-tone cel shading too. Hair and hats cast a separate shadow onto the face. Keep light direction consistent with the lamp, window or sun.
- Soft bodies: narrow shoulder into broad low belly, short thick legs and curved arms with rounded hands. Tailor the silhouette: short stocky porter versus a tall angular clerk versus a broad-chested officer. Vary head size, neck/torso ratio, hem, sleeves, cap, hair locks, lapels, cuffs, seams, folds and asymmetrical details. Changing only the tunic colour is not a new person.
- An expression is a drawing: choose eyes, brows, mouth, spacing, gaze, turn, nod and tilt together. A smug shut-eye smile can become one half-lidded eye, a compressed crooked mouth and a tilted chin on the next phrase. A listener can recoil, squint, clench a hand or look at an object without speaking. Write custom eyes/mouths through the exported dictionaries or head hooks when a preset combination is too generic.

## Framing and depth

This format lives on huge, deliberately cropped people. Close-ups often have a 400–700 px head and a second 250–450 px head at a different depth. Hats/skulls/shoulders may leave the frame; eyes and mouth stay readable. Put the main head off-centre, overlap figures or foreground props, and leave room on the side of a gesture. A low camera can crop legs while a high camera shows a table; a slight roll helps a reaction. Tilt and scale do not by themselves establish a new angle: redraw the set's perspective and the visible sides of props.

Alternate close reactions, unequal two-shots, full figures and motivated inserts. A third or more of character scenes should usually be close, but judge the story's needs. Full-figure heads still need about 140 px of height. A tiny figure belongs to a vista or a map, where the smallness communicates something. Avoid a row of equal-size, evenly spaced people or a centred actor with large empty margins.

A setting has depth, not just decoration:

1. Sky/far wall and an identifiable light source.
2. Pale distant shapes, softened by the air colour.
3. Inked architecture/ground in the acting plane, converging on a chosen vanishing point.
4. Large shadow and light shapes that cross surfaces diagonally.
5. A dark cropped foreground object, person or wall.

Use irregular clouds, varied rooftops, deep windows, cornices, plank grain and knot swirls, worn masonry, sloping ground, contact shadows and carefully placed furniture. An interior needs a ceiling/wall/floor relationship, not a grid behind people. A room may use fewer large boards rather than a busy texture. Grain means drawn wood grain inside boards; **do not add film grain, speckles or an all-over sepia/paper overlay**. The reference's warmth comes from light; the local colours remain saturated. Choose red/blue/green/gold accents, cool shadows and white faces.

The reusable whole-place painters are starting points. Move their vanishing point, repaint the light, furnish them and create story-specific architecture. A generic hall, a generic wall and a generic field alone cannot match a hand-drawn film's environment range.

## Explanation inserts

The blueprint is blue paper across a wooden desktop, with a large cropped pencil at one corner and eraser at another. The drawings are thick white-line cartoons of this film's own people/objects, not tidy boxes, circular icons or business arrows. Show the mechanism as action: two figures attempt a lift, one gets flattened, a hand changes a price tag. Cross-fade/swap/slide whole drawings. Do not progressively trace every line as chalk. Keep the same character silhouettes as the colour scenes, with hidden lines erased.

Maps have mottled blue water, parchment neutral land and dotted/stippled colour patches, saturated regions, a clear contrasting border and occasional faces/costumed figures. The regions can react and shove. A fictional schematic must be marked as such; do not present invented coastlines or borders as factual geography. Document inserts need an object that fills the frame, broad visible wording, stamps/seals and angled shadows. Fake tiny body text may serve as texture but must not carry information.

## Motion and editing

Hold drawings between distinct pose changes. A swap often anticipates by sinking/squashing for ~.05–.07s, switches at the cue, rises with slight overshoot and settles in ~.12–.16s. The total accent is short; continuous spring wobble looks wrong. A snap zoom of 15–35% often accompanies it. Keep continuous drift to a few percent over a shot. Smooth camera frames at 60 fps coexist with mouth/limb drawings held at roughly 8–12 changes/second.

Use 3–4 mouth drawings sampled from voice loudness every 4–6 frames; only a speaker talks. Blinks are occasional. Animate cause and effect: a prop is picked up, carried by the hand, dropped, hits the floor, then the witness reacts. Do not make every body part continuously float. An arrival bounce is useful for a label, not an excuse to bob all characters forever.

Hard cuts are normal. Use pushes to reactions, object inserts and explanation boards when they change what the audience knows. A 4–6 second shot should contain a real internal action, not only a pan or mouth flaps. Leave ~.2–.5s for a punchline to register; change the framing before a long hold feels dead. Compare a motion strip around the cue and a short clip, not just its best still.

## Type, sound and acceptance

No persistent subtitle band. Short fat outlined uppercase labels identify places, dates or a joke. Main labels should usually be 60–100px, with a minimum around 38px at delivery size and a generous safe margin. Keep writing clear of eyes and hands. Angle text with a prop if it belongs to it; avoid mirrored writing on flipped people. Board writing can be thinner but must remain readable.

The kit uses a −16 LUFS mix, −1.5 dBTP normalization target and music substantially below voice. The studied encode measured about −15.4 LUFS; its clipping peak is not a target. Free synthesized accompaniment is original and reusable, but simpler than professionally arranged/licensed music. Local TTS likewise lacks a human narrator's timing and character performance. Give the joke room with punctuation, short clauses, silence and selective effects.

Judge shots cold, beside another shot of the same kind: contour weight, white/shadow proportion, colour, silhouette, gesture, crop, depth and background richness. The current kit can supply those ingredients; it does not automatically supply a skilled artist's bespoke perspective, anatomy or visual comedy. Name the weakest shot and the remaining gaps. Technical QA, a passing contact sheet and a claim of stylistic intent cannot substitute for looking at the pictures.
